Meta Horizon NUX was the 2025 redesign of first-run education in Horizon Worlds. I helped replace a welcome world full of instructions with a just-in-time education strategy: let people enter a world, then teach a control or action when they had a reason to use it. The redesigned onboarding increased first-world visits by about 13% and weekly active use at day 30 by about 7% compared with the experience it replaced.

My work covered the education principles, interaction language, and the content architecture of legal notices. I worked with product design, engineering, research, legal, policy, and localization. Product design owned the final interaction and visual design; the results reflect the team's work across the experience.

First-run education

People arrived in Horizon after setting up a Quest headset. Before they could meet anyone or play, they still had world selection, consent, loading, and more than fourteen lessons to get through. Those lessons covered useful things, including controls and safety, but people were learning them before they needed them. New features kept adding more information to the welcome.

The team's proposals had focused on shortening or rearranging that curriculum. I wanted to change when the teaching happened. Someone looking for another world had a reason to learn the navigator. Someone trying to speak with their mic off had a reason to learn the mic control. Those moments gave an instruction a job it didn't have at the entrance.

The education strategy had to help people reach their first world, teach through the interaction itself, and give teams reusable components for later lessons. A controller tooltip was one application of that strategy.

Contextual instruction

VR let us attach the explanation to the thing a person needed to use. Instead of asking someone to read a floating panel, remember a button's name, and find it on their controller, the instruction could point directly to that button.

The controller education pattern combined a highlighted button, vibration, and an anchored popover. Completing the action dismissed the instruction. The popover also had a timeout and a cooldown before another one appeared. The important distinction was when the lesson became relevant: a person's state and actions determined the need for teaching, while timers kept an individual instruction from lingering.

The mic education example began “Trying to talk?” It connected an apparent intention to the mic's current state and the control that could change it. Other applications covered opening world details, settings, safety actions, and the camera. The same strategy could support different tasks without making the welcome world carry every lesson at once.

Developing the strategy

In a FigJam workshop called Antiprinciples, I asked the team to invent the worst possible welcome, then reverse its ideas. We used the exercise to work through when to interrupt and when to leave someone alone. UX research helped me frame the leadership discussion around getting people into the social experience sooner.

I developed and refined the principles with the team and worked with design and the foundations team to get the controller tooltip approved as a reusable component. That gave future education a consistent place and behavior, rather than requiring a new instructional panel for each feature.

Recording disclosure

The legal-notice work extended across Horizon OS and Worlds. Six separate sequences had accumulated, with overlapping information and different implementations. I audited those experiences and worked with Privacy Legal, Policy, and Product Counsel on their content architecture.

We separated the full disclosure from the reminder. The full explanation moved to an appropriate point in phone setup. In a world, people received a compact notice about audio recording with a Learn more route. The required information remained available, but it no longer all had to be read at the moment someone wanted to join other people.

The in-world sentence needed to cover safety and future audio features while fitting a small surface. Legal, policy, and localization worked through it with us for about two months. When the German translation ran onto a third line, I brought our localization expert and legal together to find wording that fit without losing the required meaning.

Results and adoption

The new onboarding increased first-world visits by about 13% and weekly active use at day 30 by about 7%. Those are results for the combined experience, including changes to education, interaction design, and the journey. They don't isolate the effect of a particular sentence or tooltip.

After the Horizon rollout, the Meta Quest onboarding team adopted the education strategy and its components. The work established a reusable way to teach through the situation someone was already in, with the control close enough to act on what they had just learned.

Related work

Main article: Portals

The year before, I worked on Portals, a new way to travel between experiences in Meta Horizon. New users met the portal, and its experiment counted how many of them finished their first run. Before the portal was introduced at Meta Connect 2024, I shared the portal strategy for onboarding prototypes that would teach new users how to use it.

Both projects turned on when information should appear. First-run education waited until someone needed a control. A portal showed more as someone approached it.
