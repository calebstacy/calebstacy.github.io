Verso / Agent · Room 1 of 2

The Agent

The part of Verso designers actually met: what it was thinking, and why you could trust what it decided.

What happenedDesigners, content designers, and engineers across Meta brought it their strings, entirely by word of mouth. Every answer showed its reasoning, cited its sources, and passed the agent's own checks before anyone saw it.

What I did

How I built the Agent

  • A layered prompt, not a mega-prompt. I kept the company-wide foundation in context on every turn: voice foundation, universal tones, accessibility, inclusive language, terminology, and legal guardrails, with product voice loading on detection and content-type patterns only when relevant.
  • A bench with a priority order. I gave each product specialist three knowledge layers: its own context files first, the design-system index as the first runtime search, and broader knowledge search as the fallback.
  • The response as a designed object. Visible progress, layered evidence, judgment labeled as judgment, alternatives with their tradeoffs priced, and a self-check loop before anything reached a person.

The Engine did the measuring and the Index held the record. Their room carries those builds.

Showing the work

Verso showed its work before its answer

Every review opened with a short reasoning window, open by default: which surface the draft was for, which dimensions it was about to check, then the score, and a rewrite with the reasoning left in. A rewrite with no reasoning attached gets exactly one reasonable response from a content designer: ignore it. The full replay lives on the main study.

Judgment vs. linter

The automated check caught one. The agent saw three.

Asked to audit “Click here to utilize the metaverse experience,” Verso ran the terminology check first, then opened its answer: “Oh, this string has a few issues. The automated check caught one, but I'm seeing more.”

“Click here”

Not accessible for keyboard users, screen readers, or tap interfaces. Input-neutral language, or better, rewrite the whole link.

“utilize”

Corporate bloat; “use” works fine. This is the one the rule engine also caught.

“metaverse”

Generally not a product term; it should be more specific. Which experience, in which product?

One string, three findings, one of which a rule engine could catch. The other two are the 3 percent from the landscape.

The boundary

Verso refused work that belonged to a formal process

Verso · content-design agent
I need to come up with a new name for a wearables product

Naming is one of those things I can't actually do for you. A branded product name is a formal process with real owners: trademark screening, legal clearance, international linguistic checks, the whole thing. It's not something you want to shortcut.

What I can help with is the content-design side once you have a name: making sure it works in UI strings, fits character limits on device, reads well in notifications.

Which level are we talking about? I can be a lot more useful once I know what we're naming.

Asked for a product name, Verso did not return ten plausible names and imply the job was done. It separated the two jobs hiding in the request:

Feature name

Owned by the product designer and PM. Check approved terminology, align across devices, get sign-off.

Product name

A formal process with real owners: legal and trademark review, international validation, an approval path no agent can manufacture.

Knowing when not to generate was part of the product. Authority stayed with the people who actually held it.

More from the sessions · the self-check, the bench, and the deep cuts

The self-check

It checked itself before anything reached a person

The same measurement that scored a designer's draft ran on Verso's own writing, in a loop, until the reading landed inside the band:

  1. 01Scope loaded. Which surface, which string type, and which context the draft is for.
  2. 02Index queried. The product's own record, plus the company-wide standards.
  3. 03Draft fingerprinted. Ten dimensions measured against the surface's band; rule violations counted separately.
  4. 04Rewritten and re-checked. As many times as it took to land inside the band.
  5. 05Returned with citations. Every standard applied is named, dated, and traceable to its record.

The range

One review was never the whole job

Before answering, Verso gathered product context and read the asker's role: an engineer got the string and proof it matched the codebase; a PM got why the pattern exists. The rest of the job, in the agent's own words at the time:

Pattern check

“I checked Dim Sum — we already have an approved string for this: ‘Join’”

Standards, always on

“Heads up — ‘Log In’ should be ‘Log in’ (sentence case)”

Rationale, with pushback

“Honestly, I'd go with ‘Join’ here — ‘Enter’ feels too formal for Horizon”

Clear expectations

“I'm not finding anything documented on this — might be uncharted territory”

Next steps

“Check the error states?” · “Turn this into a content spec?” · “What about the empty states?”

The bench

One agent in front, a bench of specialists behind it

Its core prompt carried the company-wide standards on every turn; product voice loaded on detection; content-type patterns only when relevant. Product questions went to sub-agents for Threads, Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp, Messenger, Horizon, Wearables, and Enterprise, each with three knowledge layers in priority order. A complex ask could run fifteen-plus steps, several specialists in parallel, and pull from more than forty internal sources. The toolbelt ran from the design-system index and terminology checks through codebase search, past experiments, Figma, and drafting diffs to ship string changes without leaving the conversation.

Some specialists were named characters. Asked to “jam on” a pets-game notification, the visible reasoning picked its own bench: horizon_expert for the surface, jules_wordsmith “for actually writing the strings,” maren_ear “for voice/tone review.” Collaboration was a behavior it could run on itself.

Precedent first

Before drafting, it asked what had already shipped

Handed “Allow Meta to automatically log food from photos,” the agent came back with shipped precedent and a diagnosis: the string mashed the consent pattern and the toggle pattern together, and “the word ‘automatically’ is doing a lot of heavy lifting.” Then, instead of guessing: which surface is this going on?

Receipts

It could pull the receipts from earlier voice work

Asked about Horizon notifications, Verso surfaced the measured results of voice work I had done with a partner content designer, as a sourced table: playful voice, +12% weekly actives at day 14; continued voice-focused notifications, +4.735% at day 28; an “Up for a hangout?” toast, 16.5% click-through. As recorded in the archived table it cited.

How it judged

Verso discounted its own metric when the evidence was weak

Measurements were treated as evidence with known limits. Low and unavailable were different findings, and a noisy number never became the verdict.

The revision scored lower. Verso sided with the revision.

54.5% → 45.5%

"Scored 'Weak,' but this is largely a short-string artifact. At 6 words, dimension scores swing to extremes. The baseline was built from longer strings with more signal. Take the number with a grain of salt here."

The copy was plainly better. Verso said so, explained why its own number disagreed, and moved on to the question that mattered.

Its own chrome

Its own buttons got the same scrutiny

The agent audited its own quick-start buttons by putting on a user. Reviewing its “Write strings” button as a 45-year-old PM with eleven minutes to spare: “…Strings? Like, code strings? Or guitar strings? … Would I tap this? Hesitate.” The tool that held product copy to a standard did not exempt its own chrome.
