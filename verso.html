Verso

Conversational agent, guidance index, and language engine at Meta

Verso

Work at Meta

Organization
Meta
Built by
Caleb
Development
2025–2026
Role
Agent · Index · Engine
Used by
Content designers and cross-functional partners
Access
Internal to Meta

I initiated and built Verso at Meta during 2025 and 2026. It was a chance to work on a question everyone seems to be asking these days: how do we get agents to write within guardrails that we define? Verso had three parts. People talked to the Agent. The Index held each product’s guidance in a form an agent could use, along with a voice fingerprint measured from the words that product had already shipped. The Engine ran content rules as code and measured a draft against that fingerprint, so the Agent could test its own wording before anyone saw it.

Content designers on Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook, Threads, Meta Quest and Horizon jammed with it. Designers and engineers across the company used it too, and they got a recommendation and the reasons behind it even when their team had no content designer. As of November 2026, designers and cross-functional partners continue to use the system at Meta.

Question
How do we get agents to write within guardrails that we define?
Problem
More product language was coming from agents, and from partners using AI, while the number of content designers shrank. A prompt file could describe our standards. It couldn’t guarantee that an agent followed them, and much of the guidance behind it was subjective and written for people.
Solution
I took everything an agent could compute out of the prompt and gave it to the Agent as something to run. Rules with a yes or no answer became code, and each product’s voice became a fingerprint measured from the words it had shipped. The judgment stayed in the conversation, and the Agent tested its own draft before it answered.
Outcome
Against the same targets, the Agent with its checks and a product’s fingerprint was much more accurate than agents built from a prompt file alone. Looking something up took 2 to 5 minutes instead of 30 to 60, and teams of two content designers handled work that had needed three or four.

System components

The three parts solved different problems, and I built each one as its own piece of work. They were powerful as an interconnected system. Each has its own article.

Agent

Main article: Verso Agent

The Agent was the part people talked to. More product language was coming from agents and from partners using AI, and partners didn’t always have a content designer to ask. I designed the Agent to jam with a content designer and to give anyone else a strong recommendation that still required content approval. Before it recommended any wording, it ran its own draft through the Engine and adjusted. It also brought research, recent work and the right person into the conversation. That prevented a lot of back and forth. Teams without a content designer got a content design perspective.

Index

Main article: Verso Index

The Index was our guidance, reorganized so an agent could use it. “Guidance” was one word for hard rules, judgment calls and advice about users, all of it written for people and scattered across documents, chats and spreadsheets. I sorted it by kind and turned every rule with a yes or no answer into a check. Voice and tone went into a fingerprint for each product, a set of measurements taken from the strings it had already shipped. Changes went to the product’s lead content designer as a diff. Much less had to sit in a prompt, and the fingerprint kept the Agent much closer to a product’s targets than a prompt file could.

Engine

Main article: Verso Engine

The Engine was the server the Agent called to check its own drafts. A large share of our guidance had a yes or no answer, yet an agent only got it as sentences in a prompt, and nobody could guarantee a rule had been followed. I ran those rules as Python functions on an MCP server I built. The same server measured a draft along a set of linguistic dimensions and compared it with the product’s fingerprint. I kept it warm so a check could happen in the middle of a conversation. The Agent held to our rules more consistently with it, and the dimensions gave us a way to explain a language choice.

How a request moved

In the connected system, a request passed through all three parts before the person saw an answer. The drafting and checking in the middle happened out of sight.

  1. Recognize the person. The Agent recognized who was asking by their employee ID and job title, and the Index supplied the standards for their product.
  2. Understand the work. The Agent read what the person brought, such as a content spec or a Figma page. It pulled in their recent work and asked what the product actually did.
  3. Draft out of sight. It generated options internally. The person never saw this first draft.
  4. Check. The Engine ran the product’s rule functions on the draft and measured it against the product’s fingerprint. A scorecard went back to the Agent.
  5. Adjust. The Agent revised its recommendation with what came back, and sometimes checked the revision as well.
  6. Answer in plain language. A content designer got options to jam on. A partner got a strong recommendation that still required content approval. Both got the reasons in plain words, and the scores stayed with the Agent.
  7. Feed changes back. If the person said a piece of guidance was wrong and explained why, the Agent wrote the change as a diff and tagged the lead content designer who owned it.

Background

Content design everywhere was becoming agentic. Agents were writing more product language, and content designers were being asked to work on the loop instead of in it, supervising what agents wrote more than writing it ourselves. At Meta, that shift arrived while the number of content designers was shrinking. Cross-functional partners relied more and more on AI to write their strings, and some of them had no content designer to ask. The guidance those strings had to meet was spread across an internal guidance site, documents, chats, spreadsheets and internal posts.

The tool people had was the prompt file. Many of the agents being built were really prompt files, sets of linear commands for a language model, and when one got something wrong, the fix was a more specific prompt. That can improve an agent. It can’t guarantee a rule was followed, because a model struggles to hold forty rules at once. A better prompt also leaves the guidance underneath it unchanged. An agent can only hold to language that isn’t subjective, and our guidance, like most guidance, was written for people. So the work that mattered was the foundation you need before you write a prompt at all, and the aim was to waste fewer tokens while getting more accurate.

Central content design was asking a legitimate question about all of this. How can we be sure? Sounding like the brand is part of a content designer’s job, and language written by agents still had to meet our standards. We needed a system that could scale and enforce those standards reliably without anyone babysitting it.

Who it was for

Content designers
Teams of two were stretched across workloads that needed three or four people, and decisions were sometimes made without being documented. They needed quick access to guidance, research and earlier decisions, someone to jam with, and help justifying a decision to partners.
Designers and engineers
They were using AI to write strings, and some had no content designer on their team. They needed a content design perspective and a recommendation they could act on, with a route to content approval.
Standards owners
Lead content designers owned each product’s guidance, and central content design owned the company-wide baseline. They needed to know that language from an agent met those standards, and they needed the say over any change to the guidance.

Development

I started Verso on my own initiative in 2025, while I was an AI lead on Horizon. The Agent came first, built on Meta’s internal agent harness, and content designers across the company were using it before any measurement was connected. The prompt was that good.

The Index and the Engine grew out of central’s question. Rules with a yes or no answer were solved long ago in code, so those could move out of the prompt. That left the editorial guidance, and voice and tone. My background is in poetry, where you learn the mechanics that produce an effect, and I went to linguistic research for the mechanics behind guidance like “be supportive.” I designed and built the agent, the taxonomy, the Index and the Engine, and I revised them with feedback from content designers as I went.

What testing showed

Prompt files
Agents built from a prompt file alone drifted from their product’s targets when I tested them. Against the same targets, the Agent with its checks and the product’s fingerprint was much more accurate.
Tone
Voice and tone turned out to be more measurable than we had assumed. That left much less to keep in a prompt.
The self-check
The Agent followed our rules more consistently when it used the server to lint its own output. I ran many A/B tests to prove it.
Kinds of guidance
The content design pages I tested mixed hard rules, judgment calls, advice about users and things to avoid. An agent received every line the same way, as a sentence in a prompt.

Ownership and collaboration

I built the system. Several groups owned the decisions around it, and I designed it to keep those decisions with them.

My part
I started the project, and I designed and built the Agent, the taxonomy, the Index and the Engine.
Content designers
They used it in real work and gave me feedback, and I revised the system with it.
Lead content designers
They owned their product’s guidance. Every proposed change went to them as a diff to approve or discuss.
Researchers
They could submit findings, which were filed in the Index as proposed changes.
Designers and engineers
They got a recommendation and the reasoning behind it. Approval of the wording stayed with a content designer.
Central content design
They owned the company-wide baseline and the call on taking anything company-wide. I partnered with them as use grew.
Meta
Meta owned the platform and the infrastructure it ran on.
Open-source authors
They wrote the language libraries the Engine’s measurements run on, including spaCy.

Use and results

Use began with content designers and spread to designers and engineers. Within about six months it had scaled beyond Horizon to teams across Meta. It was a force multiplier for the discipline in an environment where headcount was dwindling.
